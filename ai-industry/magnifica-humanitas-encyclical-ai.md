# Magnifica Humanitas — Pope Leo XIV's Encyclical on AI (May 2026)

**Source:** [Vatican: Magnifica Humanitas](https://www.vatican.va/content/leo-xiv/en/encyclicals/documents/20260515-magnifica-humanitas.html)
**Author:** Pope Leo XIV
**Date issued:** 15 May 2026
**Document type:** Papal encyclical (social doctrine)

---

## Summary

Magnifica Humanitas is Pope Leo XIV's first major social encyclical, framed as an explicit successor to Leo XIII's *Rerum Novarum* (1891) and applying the Church's social doctrine to artificial intelligence, robotics, and digital transformation. Its central claim: AI is not a neutral tool, technology is never morally neutral, and the dominant actors driving it are now private transnational firms whose power exceeds that of many governments (source: 2026-05-26-Encyclical Letter of His Holiness Leo XIV Magnifica Humanitas (15 May 2026).md). The encyclical asks who holds AI power and how it is used, calling for binding regulation, data treated as a shared good, AI alignment frameworks subject to public deliberation (not corporate choice), and slowing adoption where institutions cannot keep pace. It links AI policy to labour dignity, weapons, multilateralism, and the protection of democracy.

## Framing: Babel vs Jerusalem

The encyclical's controlling biblical image is a binary: building Babel (uniformity, self-affirmation, technology that "reaches heaven without God's blessing") versus rebuilding Jerusalem (shared responsibility, polyphonic communion, technology coordinated by all) (source: encyclical, §§7–10). Technology is "never neutral, because it takes on the characteristics of those who devise, finance, regulate and use it" (§9). The encyclical names the "Babel syndrome" as "the idolatry of profit that sacrifices the weak, a uniformity that neutralizes differences, and the pretense that a single language — even a digital one — can translate everything, including the mystery of the person, into data and performance" (§10).

## Continuity with Catholic social doctrine

Leo XIV explicitly stands the encyclical in the *Rerum Novarum* lineage (135th anniversary year) and quotes from Leo XIII, Pius XI (*Quadragesimo Anno*, subsidiarity), Pius XII, John XXIII (*Pacem in Terris*), Vatican II (*Gaudium et Spes*, *Dignitatis Humanae*), Paul VI (*Populorum Progressio*), John Paul II (*Laborem Exercens*, *Sollicitudo Rei Socialis*, *Centesimus Annus*), Benedict XVI (*Caritas in Veritate*), and Francis (*Evangelii Gaudium*, *Laudato Si'*, *Fratelli Tutti*, *Dilexit Nos*, *Dignitas Infinita*) (source: encyclical, §§29–44). The point: AI requires extending the same principles — dignity of the person, common good, universal destination of goods, subsidiarity, solidarity, social justice — rather than inventing new ones.

## What the encyclical says about AI specifically

(source: encyclical, Chapter 3, §§92–113)

**AI is "cultivated" more than "built."** Designers do not directly engineer every detail; they create frameworks within which the system "grows." Internal representations and computational processes remain unknown even to developers, creating a twofold need: deeper scientific research and moral discernment.

**AI is not human intelligence.** Imitates functions, often surpasses humans in speed and computation, but "do not undergo experiences, do not possess a body, do not feel joy or pain… do not have a moral conscience." Their "learning" is statistical adaptation, not inner growth.

**Three personal-use risks named:** (a) the ease of obtaining results weakens creativity and judgment; (b) the impression of objectivity hides the cultural assumptions of designers; (c) the simulation of human communication risks degrading the desire for real relationships.

**Society-level risks:** environmental cost (energy, water, CO₂, infrastructure), opacity of automated decisions affecting employment, credit, public services, and reputation; reinforcement of ideological bias presented as neutrality; amplification of existing economic and data-access power asymmetries.

**Governance demands:**
- Accountability "clearly defined at every stage" — design, development, deployment.
- "Robust legal frameworks, independent oversight, informed users and a political system that does not abdicate its responsibility" — not "ethics in the abstract."
- Critique of corporate-led AI alignment: "we cannot be satisfied with merely calling for the moralization of machines — the so-called 'alignment' of AI with human values — without also having the courage to insist on a further condition: the possibility of openly discussing the ethical frameworks involved and subjecting them to shared standards of social justice" (§107).
- "Data cannot be left solely in private hands"; data is the product of many contributors and must be managed as a common or shared good.
- Slowing adoption is named as an act of responsible care, not opposition to progress.

**Transhumanism and posthumanism are rejected** as narratives that treat human limit and weakness as defects to be engineered away. The encyclical insists human dignity is ontological — grounded in being created, willed and loved — and cannot be earned through performance, efficiency, or upgrade (§§51–53, citing *Dignitas Infinita*).

## Work, truth, and freedom (Chapter 4)

The encyclical treats AI's labour impact as the central social-justice question (continuation of *Laborem Exercens*) (source: encyclical, §§115–134). It warns against:

- Job insecurity, fragmented careers, and automation evaluated only by efficiency rather than worker dignity.
- An information ecosystem where "truth as a common good" is corroded by AI-generated content, hyper-personalised feeds, and the collapse of shared reference points.
- New dependencies and commercialisations of attention that constitute "new forms of slavery."
- Schools and families displaced as primary educational subjects by algorithmic mediation.

## AI weapons and multilateralism (Chapter 5)

Strong condemnation of autonomous weapons systems and AI-driven military escalation (source: encyclical, §§148–155). The encyclical names "the normalization of war," "force without limits," and "the crisis of multilateralism" as interlinked. Calls for diplomacy, the perspective of victims, and the disarming of language. Argues that AI integrated into weapons systems is not merely a technical question but a civilisational test for the international order.

## Why this matters politically

Magnifica Humanitas is the most authoritative religious framing of AI yet issued by a major global institution. It:

1. Reframes AI governance as a question of power distribution, not just safety — directly challenging the industry's preferred framing of "alignment" as a technical problem soluble by the labs themselves.
2. Provides Catholic-aligned governments (Italy, Poland, Philippines, Latin America, parts of central Europe) with a doctrinal text they can cite when legislating AI rules, labour protections, or data sovereignty.
3. Lands during the EU AI Act enforcement phase and US federal-preemption debates — gives non-tech-industry stakeholders a high-status counter-narrative.
4. Stands in deliberate tension with the US religious right's pro-acceleration stance (see [[../us-religion-and-politics/index|US Religion and Politics]]). Catholic social doctrine in this document is structurally closer to European centre-left and global-south concerns than to MAGA's tech alignment.
5. Frames data as a shared good — a doctrinal warrant for data-cooperative and data-commons movements pushing back on the lab-owned-corpus default.

## Key Takeaways

- First major papal encyclical fully dedicated to AI, robotics, and digital transformation; explicit successor framing to *Rerum Novarum* (1891)
- "Technology is never neutral" — embeds the priorities, biases, and visions of those who design, finance, and deploy it
- Power has shifted from states to transnational private firms whose resources exceed many governments — governance must catch up
- Industry-led "AI alignment" is insufficient; ethical frameworks must be publicly deliberated, not corporate-defined
- Data must be regulated as a common or shared good, not left to private ownership
- Slowing AI adoption is named as responsible care, not anti-progress
- Transhumanism and posthumanism explicitly rejected; ontological dignity is not earned by performance or upgrade
- AI weapons and the crisis of multilateralism named as a civilisational test linked to the broader culture of power
